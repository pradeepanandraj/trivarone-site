<p class="lede">
  I sat down one afternoon to see how other people describe the work I do. I
  opened four websites offering the same service, and they called it four
  different things.
</p>

<p>
  Not to copy anyone. I wanted to know what language a practice owner would run
  into if they went looking, so that I would recognise it when somebody quoted
  it back at me.
</p>

<p>
  The first called it AI SEO. The second called it AEO. The third called it LLM
  SEO in one place and AEO in another. The fourth used three different terms on
  a single page, without acknowledging anywhere that they were three names
  rather than three services.
</p>

<p>
  I closed the fourth tab thinking I had missed something. Some settled
  definition that everybody else had and I did not.
</p>

<p>
  There is no settled definition. That is the finding, and it took me a while to
  accept it.
</p>

<h2>Why the naming is a mess</h2>

<p>
  The change these words describe is about three years old. Search engines began
  answering questions directly instead of only listing pages, and a set of new
  systems began answering them without a results page at all.
</p>

<p>
  When something moves that quickly, the naming happens before the agreement.
  Several people describe the same shift at the same time, each invents a term,
  and all of them survive because each one has somebody using it. There is no
  committee. There is no standard. There is no memo you failed to receive.
</p>

<p>
  If you have found this confusing, the confusion is accurate. You are not
  behind. Here is what I found each one actually means.
</p>

<h2>SEO — search engine optimisation</h2>

<p>
  The parent term, in use since the late 1990s. Arranging a website so that
  search engines can read it, understand what it covers, and show it to people
  looking for that subject.
</p>

<p>
  Everything below is built on this. None of the newer words replaces it,
  whatever anybody tells you. A site that search engines cannot read properly
  will not be found by the newer systems either, because most of them still
  depend on a search index underneath.
</p>

<h2>Local SEO</h2>

<p>
  The oldest of the specialisations and the one with the clearest meaning. It is
  about appearing when somebody searches for a service near a place — a
  physiotherapist in a suburb, a dentist in a postcode, support coordination in
  a region.
</p>

<p>
  It works differently from ordinary SEO because the result on the page is
  different. Google shows a map with three listings above the ordinary results,
  and what puts a business into that map is not the same as what puts a page at
  the top of the list below it.
</p>

<p>Three things carry most of the weight.</p>

<ul>
  <li>
    <strong>Distance.</strong> The physical gap between the searcher and your
    front door affects whether you appear. Nobody can optimise that away, and it
    means your results differ depending on where the person searching is standing.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>The listing rather than the website.</strong> Your Google Business
    Profile is a separate thing from your website, with its own categories,
    description, photographs and reviews. A practice can have an excellent
    website and a neglected listing, and in local search the listing frequently
    matters more.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Consistency.</strong> Whether your business name, address and
    telephone number are written the same way everywhere they appear. Small
    inconsistencies accumulate into uncertainty about whether these are all the
    same business.
  </li>
</ul>

<p>
  For most practices this describes the largest part of the work that actually
  needs doing, and it has nothing whatever to do with artificial intelligence. I
  say that plainly because local search was reshaped years before AI arrived and
  the fundamentals have not changed since. Anybody selling you an AI-era service
  who has not looked at your Google Business Profile has skipped the part most
  likely to make a difference.
</p>

<h2>AEO — answer engine optimisation</h2>

<p>
  This one is older than the AI wave, which surprised me.
</p>

<p>
  It originally described getting your content into the answer box at the top of
  a Google results page — the featured snippet — and into the replies given by
  voice assistants. That was a real discipline with real techniques well before
  generative AI arrived: clear question-and-answer structure, direct answers
  stated early, content formatted so a machine could lift a definition out of it
  cleanly.
</p>

<p>
  Since roughly 2023 the term has been reused to mean getting into AI answers as
  well. So a page using AEO might mean the older thing, the newer thing, or both
  without distinguishing them. I have found the publication date of the page is
  usually a better guide than its text.
</p>

<h2>GEO — generative engine optimisation</h2>

<p>
  This is the only one of them with a formal definition behind it. It comes from
  an academic paper published in November 2023, freely readable at
  <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735</a>.
</p>

<p>
  Worth one aside before the definition. Two separate summaries told me where
  that paper came from, and they contradicted each other. Neither matched the
  paper. It took reading the first page to find that out, and it is the reason I
  now go to the source whenever a claim is going to end up in something I publish.
</p>

<p>
  <strong>What GEO means:</strong> visibility inside an AI-generated answer,
  rather than a position in a list of links. The argument in the paper is that
  traditional SEO does not transfer directly, because a generative system is not
  matching keywords — it is reading, summarising, and deciding what to cite.
</p>

<h2>What the GEO paper actually found</h2>

<p>
  The figure everybody quotes is that these methods improved visibility by up to
  forty per cent. That number is real. It was measured across a benchmark of ten
  thousand queries.
</p>

<p>
  It is also a controlled-experiment result on a test set. It is not a figure
  anybody can promise you for your website, and if somebody quotes it at you as
  though they can, that tells you something about them.
</p>

<p>What is more interesting is which methods worked and which did not.</p>

  <table>
    <thead>

      <tr>

      </tr>
    </tbody>

  </table>
</div>

<p>
  There is a plain reading of all this. What improves visibility in AI answers is
  roughly what makes writing genuinely good: specific, sourced, clear, and honest
  about where its facts came from. That is not a coincidence and it is not
  marketing. It is what the measurement showed.
</p>

<h2>The finding in that paper that matters most for a small practice</h2>

<p>
  The authors also tested what happens when every source in a set is optimised,
  and looked at the results by where each site already ranked in ordinary search.
</p>

<p>
  Lower-ranked sites gained substantially more of the answer than higher-ranked
  ones did, and the top-ranked site's share fell. The share is finite — an answer
  is one paragraph, so when every source improves they are dividing the same
  paragraph differently.
</p>

<p>
  The explanation given is straightforward. Traditional search rankings depend
  heavily on things that favour large established organisations — the number of
  other sites linking to you, the age and authority of your domain. Those are
  difficult for a small business to build. But a generative system reads the
  content of the page. It is not counting your backlinks in the same way.
</p>

<blockquote>
  <p>
    At the answer stage, a small practice competes on the quality of its text
    rather than on the authority of its domain.
  </p>
</blockquote>

<p>
  I would not overstate this. It is one finding, in one paper, in one
  experimental setup, and the authors themselves note that these systems will
  keep changing. But it is the most encouraging piece of evidence I have found
  for a small practice, and it comes from peer-reviewed research rather than from
  somebody selling a service.
</p>

<h2>LLMO — large language model optimisation</h2>

<p>
  Coined by practitioners rather than researchers, at roughly the same time as
  GEO, and overlapping with it heavily.
</p>

<p>
  Where the emphasis differs: LLMO tends to be used for whether a system
  recognises your business exists at all — knows the practice, knows what it
  does, knows where it is, and can distinguish it from a similarly named business
  somewhere else. That is a question about entity recognition rather than about
  any particular page being cited.
</p>

<p>
  The distinction matters more than it sounds. A practice can have well-written
  pages that a system will happily cite when asked about a topic, while that same
  system does not reliably know the practice itself exists as a business. Those
  are two different problems requiring two different fixes.
</p>

<h2>LLM SEO</h2>

<p>
  Used interchangeably with LLMO by most people who use it, and I have not found
  a consistent distinction between them.
</p>

<p>
  What I find interesting is the phrasing rather than the definition. LLM SEO
  keeps the letters SEO inside it. LLMO drops them.
</p>

<p>
  That is a small choice with a position embedded in it. Keeping SEO in the name
  says this is a continuation of an existing discipline. Dropping it says this is
  a new thing. The people who use each form generally do believe the
  corresponding thing, and if you notice which form somebody uses, you have
  learned something about how they think about the work before they have said a
  word about it.
</p>

<p>
  For what it is worth, my own view is that it is a continuation. But that is a
  view, not a fact, and the naming is not evidence either way.
</p>

<h2>AIO — and this is the one that caught me out</h2>

<p>
  I assumed AIO meant AI optimisation. It does, for some people.
</p>

<p>
  For others it means AI Overview optimisation — appearing specifically in the
  AI-generated summary that Google places above its ordinary results.
</p>

<p>
  Those are not the same objective. The first is a broad aim across many systems.
  The second is a specific target on one surface of one search engine. I could
  not always tell from context which was meant, and in at least one case I do not
  think the writer had decided.
</p>

<p>
  If somebody uses AIO with you, it is entirely reasonable to ask which they mean.
</p>

<h2>AI SEO — ambiguous in a way that costs money</h2>

<p>
  This is the term I use, and looking into it properly made me more careful about
  how I use it.
</p>

<p>
  It gets used to mean <em>using AI tools to do the optimisation work</em> —
  drafting content faster, clustering keywords, running technical audits at
  scale. It also gets used to mean <em>optimising so that AI systems find and
  recommend you</em>.
</p>

<p>
  Those are close to opposite propositions. The first describes how the supplier
  works internally. The second describes what you receive. A supplier could
  deliver the first perfectly while doing nothing at all about the second.
</p>

<p>
  A practice owner being sold AI SEO is entitled to ask which is on offer,
  because the answer changes what the money buys. For the avoidance of doubt on
  this site: I mean the second.
</p>

<h2>AISO — AI search optimisation</h2>

<p>
  The plainest of the abbreviations, and the one that describes its own scope
  most directly: optimising for AI search, without committing to any particular
  system or surface.
</p>

<p>
  That breadth is the whole point of it. GEO names generative systems, AEO names
  answer engines, LLMO names language models. AISO names none of them, which
  makes it the term least likely to be wrong later, and the least informative
  about what is actually being done. It is the safe word rather than the precise
  one.
</p>

<h2>AI search and AI searchability</h2>

<p>
  These two sit slightly apart from everything above, and they are worth taking
  together. Neither is a coinage with an origin to point to.
</p>

<p>
  <em>AI search</em> names the surface rather than the practice — the AI
  Overview, the AI Mode answer, the reply from ChatGPT or Gemini or Perplexity
  that somebody reads instead of a list of links. <em>AI searchability</em> names
  a property of a website: whether those systems can reach it, read it, and put
  it in front of a person who has asked a relevant question.
</p>

<p>
  Both remain in steady use among people who do this work, and I have seen
  practitioners describe themselves as working in SEO and AI searchability rather
  than choosing one of the abbreviations. That is not carelessness. When no
  abbreviation has settled, a plain phrase that describes the thing is the safer
  choice, because it cannot be misread as a claim about whose method is correct.
</p>

<h2>SGE — a name that is retired, and why it is still useful</h2>

<p>
  SGE stood for Search Generative Experience. It was Google's own name for its
  AI-generated answers during the period when the feature was being tested rather
  than fully released.
</p>

<p>
  Google later renamed it. The AI-generated summary that now appears above
  ordinary results is called an AI Overview, and the separate conversational
  version is called AI Mode. SGE, as a name, no longer refers to anything Google
  currently offers.
</p>

<p>
  The term still appears in a great deal of published material, and this is where
  it becomes useful to you. If you find a guide, an agency page or an article
  still using SGE as a current term, it was written before the rename and has not
  been revisited since. That tells you the material is at least a year or two old
  without your having to judge whether its content is right.
</p>

<p>
  In a field where a great deal of published advice is simply old, having a quick
  way to date something is worth more than it sounds. I now use it exactly that
  way. It is the fastest single check I know for whether a page has been
  maintained.
</p>

<p>
  One caution: some writers still use SGE deliberately as a shorthand for the
  whole category of AI answers, knowing perfectly well that Google retired the
  name. Context usually makes it obvious which is happening.
</p>

<h2>What actually differs, once the words are set aside</h2>

<p>
  Underneath the vocabulary, most of the work is shared. Content that answers a
  real question clearly. A site a machine can read without difficulty. Accurate
  and consistent information about the business. Being mentioned somewhere other
  than your own website. All of that serves every objective on the list.
</p>

<p>The genuine differences are narrower than the naming suggests.</p>

<p>
  The reason to hold both readings of AI SEO and AIO in mind is that the
  ambiguity is where money goes missing. A supplier can honestly say they do AI
  SEO while meaning only the first reading, and you can honestly hear the second.
</p>

<h2>What I would ask, if somebody used one of these words at me</h2>

<p>
  Not which term is correct. Nobody has settled that, and anybody who tells you
  it is settled is describing a preference rather than the state of the field.
</p>

<p>I would ask three things.</p>

<ul>
  <li>
    <strong>What will you actually do?</strong> An answer naming specific work on
    specific surfaces is useful whatever it is called. An answer that mostly
    defends the acronym is not.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Where will I be able to see it?</strong> Some of this work is visible
    to you directly — a listing you can look at, pages you can read, an answer
    you can go and ask a system for yourself. Some of it is not. Know which you
    are buying.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>What can you not do?</strong> Anybody working honestly in this field
    can name several things. Distance from the searcher. The age of your
    business. Whether a system happens to answer the same question differently
    tomorrow. A supplier who cannot name a single limitation has either not
    thought about it or is not telling you.
  </li>
</ul>

<h2>The question underneath all of it</h2>

<p>
  Whatever word is used, the same thing sits underneath. When somebody searches
  for what you do rather than for your name, do you appear?
</p>

<p>
  You can answer that yourself in about thirty seconds. Search your practice
  name. Then search what you actually do, the way a stranger would phrase it.
  Signed out, on a phone. Notice whether you appear at all, rather than where you
  rank.
</p>

<p>
  If the first search works and the second does not, the acronym does not matter.
  The gap does.
</p>
